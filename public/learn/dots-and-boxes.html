How to play Dots and Boxes: strategy

Dots and Boxes looks like a simple pencil-and-paper game, but it hides real strategy. This guide covers the board, the rules and who moves first, then safe moves, chains, the double-cross and counting.

Try these ideas on a 4×4 board.

Play Dots and Boxes free

The board and who goes first

Dots and Boxes starts with a square grid of dots and no lines. On BoardGameSite you can play 3×3, 4×4, 5×5 or 6×6 boxes (a 4×4 board has 5 dots on each side). Player 1 moves first; against the computer, that’s you.

The rules

  1. Take turns drawing one line between two dots that are next to each other, across or down.
  2. Close a box by drawing its fourth side, and it’s yours.
  3. Closing a box earns another turn, so one turn can take several boxes in a row. You must still draw a line at the end.
  4. When every line is drawn, the player with more boxes wins. On a 4×4 or 6×6 board, an equal split is a draw.

Strategy: safe moves

Early on, any line that doesn’t make the third side of a box is safe: it gives nothing away. Avoid drawing the third side of a box, because your opponent simply takes it. As long as safe lines are left, use them.

Strategy: chains and loops

Eventually every remaining line gives something away. By then the board has split into chains, rows of boxes where taking one lets you take the next, and sometimes loops, chains that join back on themselves. When you must open something, open the shortest chain, so your opponent gains as little as possible.

Strategy: the double-cross

When your opponent opens a long chain, it’s tempting to take every box. But then you must draw another line, which opens the next chain for them. Instead, take all but the last two boxes, then draw the line at the far end that leaves those two boxes as a pair. Your opponent takes the two boxes, but then they must open the next chain. Giving away two boxes can win you a whole chain of five or six.

In a loop, the same trick costs four boxes instead of two, so think before you use it there.

Strategy: count the chains

Near the end, the player who has to open the first long chain usually loses, because the other player keeps control with double-crosses. Count the long chains (three boxes or more) before the safe moves run out. Sometimes giving away a box or two early, to change who runs out of safe moves first, decides the game.

A useful rule of thumb from expert play: the first player wants the number of dots plus the number of long chains to be even, and the second player wants it odd. So on a 4×4-box board (25 dots) the first player is hoping for an odd number of long chains. You don’t need to master it to win, but counting chains at all will put you ahead of most opponents.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Drawing the third side of a box while safe lines are still left.
  • Opening a long chain when a short one was available.
  • Always taking every box offered, instead of using the double-cross.
  • Not counting the chains until it’s too late to change them.

Dots and Boxes words to know

  • Safe line: a line that doesn’t make the third side of any box.
  • Chain: a row of boxes where taking one box sets up the next.
  • Loop: a chain that joins back on itself.
  • Double-cross: declining the last two boxes of a chain so your opponent has to move next.
  • Control: being the player who isn’t forced to open the long chains.

Dots and Boxes FAQ

Who invented Dots and Boxes?

It was first described in 1889 by the French mathematician Édouard Lucas, who called it la pipopipette.

Is it better to go first or second?

It depends on the board size and on who gains control of the chains, not on going first. Good chain counting matters far more than who starts.
